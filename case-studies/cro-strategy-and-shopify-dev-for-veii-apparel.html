Context

Veii Apparel is a community-driven activewear brand selling performance hoodies, sweatshirts, and joggers through their own Shopify store. Around mid-2024, I was hired to be their CRO Strategist and Shopify Front-End Developer. The goal was to optimize the store's conversion rates and enhance the overall user experience. I mostly used Microsoft Clarity to get Veii's customer behavioral data.

The project spanned approximately three to four months, starting in the summer, which posed unique challenges given the seasonal nature of their cold-weather product catalog.

Impact

Five initiatives were scoped, built, and measured across the engagement. Each enhancement was tracked over a two-week window.

UI Enhancement Outcome
Result 1: Product Page Redesign and CTA Banner (+0.3 pts CR lift, recalled)

Clarity data showed the size chart was buried below the fold, meaning users arrived at the add-to-cart point without having resolved their primary sizing question. Bringing the chart forward and adding a CTA banner to catalogue page templates produced a combined 0.3 percentage point lift in conversion rate across the two-week measurement window.

Result 2: Reward and Loyalty System (+1 pp CR lift, +10% revenue, recalled)

A loyalty system was collaboratively scoped to target repeat purchase behavior rather than new visitor acquisition. Under summer conditions with a cold-weather catalog, retention had more room to move than acquisition. The result was the highest-impact outcome of the project: approximately 1 percentage point CR increase and a 10% revenue lift within the measurement window.

Result 3: Navigation Updates (no measurable impact)

The desktop navigation update and catalogue dropdown were both shipped but produced no measurable change in conversion rate. TikTok-referred traffic typically lands directly on product pages rather than navigating from the top of the site, which likely limited the reach of these changes. The desktop navigation update reflected stakeholder preference rather than a behavioral data signal.

Research & Discovery

Microsoft Clarity was the sole research tool used throughout the project, installed by the founder before the engagement began. Session recordings and heatmap data surfaced three consistent behavioral patterns that shaped the prioritization of work:

Mid-Page Drop-Off

The majority of visitors were leaving between the 50 and 75 percent scroll depth, suggesting friction or a loss of intent before reaching the CTA.

Rage Clicks on Product Pages

Concentrated rage-click activity pointed to user frustration, most likely caused by sizing information being unavailable at the moment users needed it to commit to a purchase.

Low Catalogue Exploration

No formal user interviews or surveys were conducted. All design decisions were grounded in Clarity's behavioral data.

Key Insights
My Insight

Bringing out the size chart outside to the open, lessen the friction for visitors to find the sizing information; which is a critical factor for purchase decision.

My Insight

Targeting existing buyers through the reward system had more leverage, and the results reflected that.

My Insight

The desktop navigation update was a stakeholder preference call rather than a data-driven one; the two-week results confirmed that it did not move the needle.

Design Iterations

Each change followed a staging-first workflow: build in staging, review and approve with the founder, document the change, push live, then measure over a two-week window before prioritising the next initiative. This made the process methodical even when the contract was short and the measurement windows were limited.

The scope of the engagement expanded mid-contract when the founder realised the role included front-end Shopify development capability. The original brief was behavioral data analysis and friction-point fixes. By the end, the work also included a loyalty system and a campaign landing page, both of which went beyond the original CRO mandate.

Reflection

The clearest lesson I got from this contract was on setting expectations right off the bat. No amount of UX improvement was going to overcome the structural reality of selling cold-weather gear in the summer time.

Moving forward, I'd communicate more clearly about the nature of CRO work and the realistic expectations for measuring timelines. Bad data is still good data, behavioral change takes time to surface in metrics, and the role is to remove friction between intent and purchase, not to manufacture conversion overnight.

If I'd taken this stance earlier in a project, I believe it would actually protect the quality of the work and the working relationship I have with my clients.
