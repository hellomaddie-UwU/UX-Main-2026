The Duck Stops Here: Designing Kuushy’s Posture-Coaching App

Contract Duration

7.5 Weeks

Industry

AI & Technology Health & Wellness

Output Type

Mobile App
A 7.5-week engagement as sole UX/UI Designer for Kuushy, an AI-powered smart cushion that coaches back posture. The brief shifted multiple times as a first-time founder introduced new ideas mid-development, making it the job to translate those ideas into structured, buildable UX while protecting the original scope.

Context

Kuushy is a smart, Bluetooth-enabled cushion designed to improve sitting posture, developed by an independent startup team shortly after their Kickstarter campaign was funded. At the start of the engagement, the product had no finalized branding: the team was still switching between blue and orange as a primary palette, and the logo in use was not the official version. The brief itself continued to shift throughout the project, as the CEO introduced new feature ideas mid-development without always engaging with the UX reasoning behind the flows those ideas required. This made establishing and holding a stable design direction the central challenge of the engagement, more than any single screen or flow.

Research & Discovery

Discovery happened through these inputs.

Requesting the cushion’s technical specifications before designing the sensor visualization surfaced how posture data was actually being detected and reported, which shaped a feedback UI grounded in real device behavior rather than a generic loading or status icon.

Competitor research on existing exercise apps informed the “next move” and target-area UI conventions proposed for the exercise-trigger flow, since those patterns are a common expectation in the category.

With no existing brand materials at project start, an initial branding direction was built directly from the brief’s own description as a working starting point; the client later overrode the color choice in favor of orange once he had something concrete to react to.

Design Iterations

The first rendition was more exploratory; the final version was heavily simplified to match the leaner, orange-led brand direction the client ultimately approved.

Moved from an initial blue-leaning direction, chosen as a designer judgment call fitting a health-related product, to orange, a direct client preference introduced once branding materials existed to react to.

Added mid-project at the client’s request. A personalized, per-mistake-type exercise routine with a next-move UI was proposed and rejected; a simplified, generic version of the trigger flow was carried into the final journey map.

A full chick-to-grown-duck growth arc was designed, but the client kept only the chick stage in the shipped version to reduce MVP development scope.

A dedicated Settings screen, end-of-session stats, avatar selection (despite being part of the original brief), and an upcoming-moves/target-area indicator were all designed and subsequently rejected by the client during review.

The Results
Impact

No post-launch metrics were shared by the client after the engagement closed, and Webpuppies had no visibility into product performance following handoff, so outcome here is described through delivery and process discipline rather than measured impact. The engagement ended with a locked, buildable design direction and a working exercise animation library, despite starting with no finalized branding and a brief that changed shape multiple times mid-project.

Key Results
Result 1: A Mid-Project Feature Request Became a Structured Flow Proposal

When the CEO asked for an exercise-trigger feature partway through the project, it was translated into a specific UX proposal: a flow that would detect recurring posture mistakes and automatically surface a matching corrective exercise, using a “next move” pattern common to exercise apps. The client rejected the personalized, mistake-type-specific version and the next-move UI, but a simplified version of the exercise trigger flow was still incorporated into the final product journey.

Result 2: A Stable Design System Absorbed a Late Brand Change Without Rework

The client’s decision to move from a blue-leaning palette to orange came after component patterns were already established. Because those patterns were built as platform-agnostic design system elements rather than one-off screens, the color change could be applied without reworking the underlying structure.

Result 3: Sensor-Based UI Was Grounded in Real Hardware Behavior

Before designing the posture-feedback visualization, the cushion’s technical specifications were requested from the client. This kept the sensor visualization tied to how the hardware actually detected and reported posture data, rather than to an assumed or generic representation.

Features
FEATURE 1
Onboarding & Calibration Flow

Walks first-time users through pairing and calibrating the cushion before use. Sequenced last in the design process so it could be built against the final approved brand and real product photography instead of placeholders, avoiding a rework cycle when the brand direction changed.

FEATURE 2
Avatar Growth Mechanic (Chick to Duck)

Gives users a visible, ongoing signal of consistent posture behavior. On first visit to the companion screen, a walkthrough asks the user to demonstrate at least one cumulative hour of good posture; completing it hatches an egg into the chick mascot. A full grown-duck stage was designed and explored, but the client chose to keep the chick as the shipped version to simplify development scope for the MVP.

Reflection

Requesting the cushion’s hardware specifications before designing sensor-dependent UI kept the feedback visualization grounded in real device behavior instead of assumption. Sequencing onboarding last also paid off directly because it meant the flow could be built once, against the final brand and real product photography, instead of being reworked after later brand changes. The platform-agnostic design system held up the same way, absorbing the late color change without structural rework.

What remained unresolved was whether the simplified versions of key flows actually held up for real users. The exercise-trigger flow shipped as a single generic trigger rather than the personalized, per-mistake-type routine originally proposed, and avatar selection, despite being part of the original brief, was cut entirely. Because no post-launch data was shared after handoff, it was never confirmed whether those simplifications served users well or left real gaps in how effectively they could self-correct their posture.

Get a clearer read on the founder’s experience level before locking process expectations. This was the CEO’s first MVP, built shortly after his Kickstarter was funded, and that context would have changed how UX rationale was presented and how firmly certain flows were held during review.
